S1.1. Hapax legomena and rare glyphs within word types

Because of the Naibbe cipher’s handling of plaintext bigrams, about half of all Naibbe

cipher word types in ciphertexts approaching the length of Voynich B appear only once,

consistent with the VMS (Zandbergen 2024). What’s more, the Naibbe cipher includes

the rare glyphs <x> and <g> as a proof of concept for how a substitution cipher could

accommodate rare Voynichese glyphs as exhibited by the VMS.



S1.2. Text interspersed within or on either side of an illustration

Because Naibbe ciphertext tokens and word types represent plaintext unigrams and

bigrams, the plaintext message is broken up into many individual chunks anyway,

making it straightforward to continue a sequence of tokens after some mid-sequence

interruption, such as an intrusive illustration. This kind of wrapping around, and even

through, illustrations is a hallmark of VMS text. While the unigram or bigram nature of

Naibbe tokens makes them less than informative as individual illustration labels, a

limitation discussed in the main text’s Section 4, it also means that the Naibbe cipher

can replicate what seem to be unusual clusters of VMS “labels,” such as the two lines of

tokens cutting through the bathing illustration in folio f75v.



S1.3. Asymmetrical word pairings

Much like many natural languages, the VMS exhibits asymmetrical word pairings (aka

skewed pairs), in which for words A and B, the word pair AB occurs more often than BA

(Caruana, Layfield, and Abela 2022). Naibbe ciphertexts do the same thing, with some

word pairs occurring more than an order of magnitude more often than their reverses.




                                                                                          2
These pairings are most often pairs of unigram word types that encrypt asymmetrical

pairs of letters in Latin and Italian, the most common being U and M, as UM is a

common Latin word ending and a more common bigram than MU (S1 Table 1).



S1.4. Smooth, orderly handwriting

With practice, the Naibbe cipher can provide for smooth, orderly, and seemingly error-

free writing akin to what is observed in the VMS. For the most part, scribes are simply

copying down prescribed strings of Voynichese glyphs. In experimental encipherment

sessions—including the one that resulted in the main text’s Figure 5, a ciphertext

encrypting some of Julius Caesar’s De bello Gallico (Holmes 2025)—I have found that

as long as the scribe is accustomed to writing the Voynichese glyph set, logging the

prescribed glyph string in short-term memory and then copying it down often results in

orderly writing, especially after doing an initial scratch-paper encryption.

       Scratch-paper encryption also provides a mechanism for the most complex

ligatures seen in the VMS, most often in the portions known as Voynich A, as seen with

the flamboyant combined treatment of the glyph <t> within <ctho> <cthey> on line 12

of folio f8r. The easiest way to create such a ligature is to do an initial encryption, spot

two <cth> glyphs that happen to be in adjacent ciphertext tokens, and then perform the

ligature while copying down the final text. It is both a complication and a flourish,

consistent with the work of an experienced scribe.



S1.5. Clustering of similar words

Puzzlingly, the VMS can exhibit clusters of words with similar structures, including

identical prefixes (Timm 2015; Timm 2016). Naibbe ciphertexts can do the same,

thanks to the word types that encrypt plaintext unigrams (S1 Table 2). For example,




                                                                                               3
because many of the primary table’s word types for plaintext unigrams begin with

<qok>, if a series of plaintext unigrams happens to be generated during respacing, the

single likeliest outcome of the default encryption procedure is that at least some of the

unigrams will start with <qok>. These kinds of <qok> clusters can be seen in the

experimental Naibbe ciphertext in Figure 5 of the main text.

       With Latin and Italian plaintexts, we would generally expect to see this kind of

clustering in areas of the plaintext with high concentrations of the letters L-T, as these

letters tend to have fairly high occurrence rates and are all adjacent to each other in the

alphabet. The construction of the Naibbe cipher’s tables is meant to emulate the

possibility—echoing the Polygraphia III cipher—that at least some VMS word types are

generated by listing out identical prefixes and then modifying suffixes on a letter-by-

letter basis (Trithemius 1564; Hermes 2022). To a truly eerie degree, the frequencies of

common Voynich B word types lend themselves remarkably well to a Naibbe-style 6-

table grouping, with 5 of the 6 Naibbe tables’ unigram options consisting mostly of

word types with one of 5 conserved beginnings—<qok>, <ok>, <qot>, <ot>, and

<olk>—and a highly regularized scheme of varying suffixes (S1 Table 2).

       The Naibbe tables’ unigram word types also allow for what Timm and Schinner

(2020) called context-dependent self-similarity: the VMS property that pairs of common

words on any given page tend to resemble each other. If a Naibbe scribe used one cipher

table especially often while encoding the unigrams on a given page—either as the

primary table within the cipher’s normal card-drawing procedure or as a non-random

preference—that table’s structurally similar word types would become a sizable fraction

of the page’s overall word types.

S1.6. Repetition of identical words

To the extent that Naibbe ciphertext word types repeat (0.18±0.04% of the time, lower

than observed VMS rates), this repetition can occur in at least three different ways:



         1.     Letter duplication within a plaintext word (e.g., radii, tutti, anno).

         2.     The end of one plaintext word and the beginning of the next plaintext

                word happen to be the same letter.

         3.     The encoding of Roman numerals, especially for C, I, M, and X.



In all three cases, the likeliest way to get a string of repeating words is for those words

to encode plaintext unigrams with a conserved table, often the primary or a secondary

table.

         For example, based purely on a comparison between their proportional

frequencies within Voynich B and the word type frequencies needed within the Naibbe

tables, I constructed the Naibbe tables so that the word types <shedy> and <qokedy>

both stand for a unigram I (in 𝛼 and 𝛽1, respectively). The additive form of the Roman

numeral 4, IIII, was still in use in medieval Europe (Burnett 2020), and in principle, one

could encode a plaintext IIII using the Naibbe cipher as something like <shedy>

<qokedy> <shedy> <qokedy> or even as <qokedy> <qokedy> <qokedy> <qokedy>.

While this work does not purport to be a decryption of the VMS—or even confirmation

that the VMS is a ciphertext—it is intriguing that this method can generate the phrase

<shedy> <qokedy> <shedy> seen in folios 83 and 84, as well as one of the VMS’s most

dramatic instances of word repetition: the four <qokedy> in a row in line 13 of folio

f75r.




                                                                                              5
       In addition, word repetition from one line to the next may occur through the

non-random use of previous lines of ciphertext as a basis for habitually encrypting the

next line of plaintext. For example, if a scribe used the word <chedy> to encrypt a

plaintext unigram E in one line and was then faced with another plaintext unigram E in

the next line, the scribe may simply recycle the <chedy> assignment without going

through the formal card-drawing procedure, similar to the “self-citation” process that

Timm and Schinner (2020) invokes.

       In principle, it should be possible to increase a Naibbe ciphertext’s rate of word

repetition to VMS-like levels (0.8–0.9%) by encrypting a Latin or Italian plaintext that

contains a large proportion of Roman numerals, such as a timeline or diary with a series

of entries that all include various years. In this case, one would expect higher repetition

rates for ciphertext word types in the primary table that encrypt the unigram versions of

C, I, M, and X, the letters most likely to be repeated multiple times within a single

Roman numeral.



S1.7. Word similarity networks

Timm and Schinner (2020) reported that unlike natural languages, the word types within

the VMS exhibit a deep connection between token frequency and the number of similar

tokens. When analyzed as a network, where individual word types represent nodes that

are connected by an edge if the two words differ by just one glyph (i.e., a Levenshtein

distance of 1, after Levenshtein (1966)), Timm and Schinner (2020) also reported that

VMS word types group into four broad domains: words similar to <daiin>; words

similar to <ol>; words similar to <chedy>; and a catch-all domain for everything else.

The word types within Naibbe ciphertexts exhibit similarity networks whose

connectivity and domains are strikingly reminiscent of the VMS. The word types within




                                                                                            6
a Naibbe ciphertext encrypting 32,000 letters of a Latin “alchemical herbal” (Ponzi

2023) exhibit a network structure (S1 Figure 1) that resembles the one reported by

Timm and Schinner (2020) for the entire VMS, with four broad domains of word types

and a longest network path of 21, identical to the VMS. In addition, the Naibbe word

network’s broad structure and connectivity resemble those of Voynich B, as reported in

the supplementary materials of Timm and Schinner (2020).



S1.8. Topic-dependent variation in word type frequencies

Through the lens of the Naibbe cipher, the VMS topic modeling results of Reddy and

Knight (2011) and Sterneck, Polish, and Bowern (2021) may indicate the detection of

ciphertext word types that encode plaintext unigrams and bigrams whose frequencies

vary by plaintext topic. For example, Latin herbals often include the word herbe so may

include a high frequency of the bigram HE or the unigram B relative to texts on other

subjects, while astronomical treatises on the moon (lunae in Latin) may include a

relatively high frequency of the bigram LU.

       I provide an initial demonstration of this effect with the Naibbe cipher by

encrypting a 32,000-letter Latin plaintext where each half is about a single known topic

and then analyzing the word type frequencies across each half of the ciphertext. The

first 16,000 letters of the plaintext come from the Latin “alchemical herbal” (Ponzi

2023), while the second half is the first 16,000 letters of Grosseteste’s astronomical

treatise De sphaera (King 2008). I find that ciphertext word types’ frequencies can vary

considerably between the two sections, with word types shared between the two

sections varying in their per-section frequencies by an order of magnitude or more and

some word types appearing only in one section or the other (S1 Table 3). The most

“relevant” words for a given section, as weighed by their overall and section-specific




                                                                                         7
frequencies, correspond to frequently used, topic-specific plaintext nouns and verbs.

Within the herbal section, for instance, many of the most relevant word types encode E,

B, V, H, and ER: seemingly picking up on the herbal’s frequent use of both the words

herbe and herba and words such as vino, vivi, and pulveriza that happen to all contain

the letter V.

        I confirm and expand on these results by performing two full topic modeling

analyses of both Voynich B and a ~21,000 Naibbe ciphertext created by encrypting this

section’s standard 32,000-letter plaintext, a four-part plaintext made of equal parts

Divina Commedia, Natural History, De sphaera, and the 14th-century alchemical

herbal. Taking after Sterneck, Polish, and Bowern (2021), I perform a latent Dirichlet

allocation (LDA) analysis, using Enderle (2017)’s GUI for MALLET (McCallum

2002). Using a custom Python script, I also perform k-means clustering on a principal

components analysis (PCA) of both Voynich B and the Naibbe ciphertext, after having

numerically represented each word in each document using term frequency-inverse

document frequency (TF-IDF) vectorization within Microsoft Excel. The optimal

number of k-means clusters is determined by silhouette score (Rousseeuw 1987), with a

minimum cluster number of 3 and a maximum of 10, for ease of broad interpretability.

        Consistent with the findings of Sterneck, Polish, and Bowern (2021), LDA

largely fails to recover topics in either Voynich B or the Naibbe ciphertext because

LDA tends to create a topic consisting of globally common word types—such as

<chedy>, <shedy>, and <ol>—and then assigns most subsets of a given document to it.

However, also consistent with Sterneck, Polish, and Bowern (2021), topic modeling

based on TF-IDF detects meaningful clusters of word types within Voynich B that are

associated with well-characterized sections of the VMS (S1 Figure 2a), with a finer




                                                                                         8
structure and a greater number of clusters preferred when Voynich B word types are

parsed by both section and the hands of Davis (2020)’s five scribes (S1 Figure 2b).

       Intriguingly, when the Naibbe ciphertext is broken into fourths—as if each

underlying plaintext section were a visually distinct section within a synthetic VMS—

and then subjected to the same analysis, Naibbe word types show meaningful clustering

based on the identity of the underlying plaintext section (S1 Figure 2c). The most

informative words within a given section per TF-IDF are either plaintext bigrams that

are diagnostic of the differences between Latin and Italian or plaintext bigrams

associated with section-abundant, topic-specific nouns and verbs.

       For example, TF-IDF suggests that the 4 most informative words within the

Divina Commedia section of the Naibbe ciphertext are <lcheedal>, <lcher>, <lcheeoy>,

and <lcheaiin>, all of which are rare ways of encrypting CH, which is abundant in

Italian (Divina Commedia) and rare in Latin (the rest of the plaintext). Similarly, the 5

most informative words per TF-IDF within the De sphaera section—<opeeo>, <oteed>,

<rees>, <ydy>, and <teeey>—translate to US, US, OT, CI, and RC, respectively,

picking up on the section’s uniquely frequent use of topic-specific plaintext words such

as circulus (circle) and motu/motus (movement).

       These results conclusively show that Naibbe ciphertexts can exhibit topic-

dependent variation in ciphertext word frequencies, in a manner consistent with the

findings reported for the VMS by groups including Reddy and Knight (2011) and

Sterneck, Polish, and Bowern (2021).



S1.9. Morphological complexity

Lindemann (2022) suggested two metrics for morphological complexity by which the

VMS could be compared to natural languages: the moving-average type-token ratio




                                                                                            9
(MATTR), and the most common words measure (MCW). The MATTR of a text

measures the average number of unique word types within a rolling window of a token

length that Lindemann (2022) fixes as 1,000 tokens. The MCWN of a text is the fraction

of tokens occupied by the N commonest word types; Lindemann (2022) finds that

languages show the most variance when N = 70.

       Naibbe ciphertexts reliably come close to replicating the VMS’s observed

MATTR and MCW70 values. Naibbe ciphertexts reliably achieve an MCW70 value of

0.44±0.01, consistent with the VMS’s reported range in Lindemann (2022) of ~0.40-

0.44. With a MATTR of 0.56±0.01, Naibbe ciphertexts overshoot what Lindemann

(2022) reported for the VMS (0.49-0.53). However, Naibbe ciphertexts sit closer to the

VMS than the Tanach Bereshit, Rettorica, Codex Suprasliensis, and Mabinogion,

Lindemann (2022)’s reference manuscripts (S1 Figure 3).

       Timm and Schinner (2024) note that in substitution ciphers where the text is

change the situation. A Naibbe ciphertext encrypting the first ~31,000 letters of Dante’s

Divina Commedia replicates Timm and Schinner (2024)’s reported absence of periodic

autocorrelation peaks within the “Stars” section of the VMS, folios f103r through f116r

(S1 Figure 4).

Supplementary Material 2: Voynichesque

Within Voynichesque, VMS-present combinations of slots 0-3 of Zattera (2022)’s slot

alphabet generate the full list of options for the first cipher alphabet (Alphabet 1).

Similarly, slots 4-7 generate the full options for the second alphabet (Alphabet 2), and

slots 8-10 generate the full options for the third alphabet (Alphabet 3). During each run

of Voynichesque, each of the three full lists of alphabet options is randomly subsampled

to generate a 26-letter alphabet, subject to tuning parameters that govern how many

letters in the alphabet are encrypted with strings of length 1, 2, 3, or 4. The terminal <y>

glyph in Zattera (2022)’s slot 11 is treated as a null, applied to the ends of ciphertext

words at a globally set probability. To mimic the common VMS word type <daiin>, we

also hold that if an Alphabet 3 glyph string encrypts a unigram letter, a null <d> is

appended to the beginning of the glyph string. We define various rules that govern: how

alphabets are generated; which alphabets can be used to encode unigrams and bigrams;

and the rules that govern those alphabets’ applications. Trigrams are encrypted with all

three cipher alphabets, applied left-to-right in 1-2-3 order.

       At first glance, Voynichesque ciphertext resembles the repetitive quality of the

VMS and replicates some of the VMS phenomenology, such as the proximity of

similar-looking word types (S2 Figure 2; see <daiiny> <daiiiny> <daiiin> in the second

line of ciphertext). While Voynichesque does not mimic the VMS with high fidelity, it

does provides a framework in which we can map plaintext letters onto VMS-present,

grammar-valid strings of Voynichese glyphs while also reproducing the VMS’s token

and word type length distributions. We can then tune Voynichesque and generate

random variants of the cipher.

S2.1. Generation of cipher alphabets

For each run of Voynichesque, we randomly subsample each cipher alphabet’s full list

of options based on the options’ glyph lengths as reported by Zattera (2022), using four

natural-number parameters that must add up to 20 (i.e., discretizing percentages to the

nearest 5%, to mimic an analog probability distribution). Each parameter determines the

percentage of cipher alphabet options that must be a specific glyph length according to

Zattera (2022). We typically set these four parameters to 3:9:7:1, based on the average

observed distribution of VMS-present glyph strings among all combinations of Zattera

slots 0–3, 4–7, and 8–10.

       During each run of Voynichesque, each cipher alphabet’s options of the same

glyph length are assigned a randomly generated number between 0 and 1 and are then

ranked. We take whatever number of top-ranked glyph strings are required to fulfill the

cipher alphabet’s total number of required strings of that length.

       For example, if the four alphabet parameters are set to 3:9:7:1, each cipher

alphabet must have 15% of their options be of glyph length 1, 45% of glyph length 2,

35% of glyph length 3, and 5% of glyph length 4. Because Voynichesque was originally

designed for English, there are 26 alphabet letters, so our randomly generated cipher

alphabet must have 0.15 × 26 ~ 4 options with glyph length 1. We then randomly rank

all the length-1 options within each cipher alphabet’s full list of options and use only the

top 4. We perform the same operation for length-2, length-3, and length-4 options. Once

we set a randomly generated 26-string cipher alphabet, we then randomly assign each

string within the alphabet to a plaintext letter by generating a random number between 0

and 1 for each of the 26 strings, ranking these numbers, and then making plaintext letter

assignments based on rank (where A = 1, B = 2, etc.).




                                                                                          20
       Because we are subsampling each cipher alphabet’s full list of options, we are

restricting the total number of word types we can generate during a given run to a

fraction of what is observed in the VMS, with some knock-on effects on calculated first-

order entropy and conditional character entropy. However, this setup also allows us to

efficiently generate large-number statistics, which at a minimum provides confidence in

the directional signals gleaned from Voynichesque.



S2.2. Parametrization of Voynichesque

To probe the relationship between the nature of a plaintext and the VMS’s statistical

properties in the context of a VMS-mimic substitution cipher, I have parameterized the

implementation of Voynichesque. Every major decision point in the encipherment

procedure—including the generation of cipher alphabets, the alphabets’ mappings to

plaintext letters, and the distribution of plaintext n-gram lengths—can be governed by

comparing the value of a manually set parameter with a random number on a token-by-

token basis.



S2.2.1. Probability of generating “Voynichesque A” and “Voynichesque B” word types

We define a “Voynichesque A” word type as a plaintext bigram that is encrypted by

applying Alphabet 2 to the bigram’s first letter and Alphabet 3 to the bigram’s second

letter. By contrast, a “Voynichesque B” word type is generated by applying Alphabet 1

to the bigram’s first letter and Alphabet 3 to the bigram’s second letter. This setup is

meant to qualitatively mimic the presence of and general structure of word types within

Voynich A and Voynich B (aka Currier A and Currier B).

       We can control the proportions of these bigram word types through use of a

globally set parameter between 0 and 1. For every token of ciphertext, we generate a




                                                                                           21
random number between 0 and 1 and compare it with the globally set parameter. If the

random number is greater than the parameter and the token encrypts a bigram, we use

the Voynichesque A word type. If the random number is equal to or less than the

parameter for a bigram, we use the Voynichesque B word type.



S2.2.2. Probability of using a given alphabet to encrypt a plaintext unigram

Within Voynichesque, either Alphabet 2 or Alphabet 3 can be used to encrypt a

plaintext unigram. We set a global parameter between 0 and 1 and then compare that

parameter to a randomly generated number between 0 and 1 on a token-by-token basis.

If the random number is less than or equal to our parameter, we choose Alphabet 2; if

not, we choose Alphabet 3.



S2.2.3. Probability of applying <y> as a null suffix

We set a global parameter between 0 and 1 that determines the probability of appending

a null suffix <y> to a ciphertext token. On a token-by-token basis, we generate a

random number between 0 and 1 and compare its value to the <y> parameter. If the

random number is less than our parameter, we append a <y> null suffix. If not, we do

not append a <y>.



S2.2.4. Distribution of plaintext n-gram lengths

Within Voynichesque, plaintexts are stripped of their original spacing and then respaced

into a mix of unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams. Two globally set parameters, each

between 0 and 1, control the probabilities that a given insertion of a space creates a

unigram, bigram, or trigram. The parameter u controls the probability of unigram




                                                                                         22
creation; the parameter t controls the probability of trigram creation. The probability of

bigram creation b is defined as:

                                      𝑏 = 1 − (𝑡 + 𝑢)                                (1)

We also require that u + b + t = 1 and that u, b, and t all be non-negative. To respace the

plaintext, a random number rn is generated between 0 and 1 for the nth token and then

compared against the following quantities:

       ●       If rn < u, insert a space into the remaining unspaced plaintext 1 letter

               after the end of the previous token.

       ●       If u ≤ rn < u + b, insert a space into the remaining unspaced plaintext 2

               letters after the end of the previous token.

       ●       If rn ≥ u + b, insert a space into the remaining unspaced plaintext 3 letters

               after the end of the previous token.



S2.3. Logging Voynichesque outputs

Within the framework outlined above, I can set tuning parameters, generate variants of

Voynichesque because of its Latin language, length, and VMS-like subject matter

S2.4. Decrypting Voynichesque ciphertexts

When reading a Voynichesque ciphertext word left to right, the longest continuous

sequence of Voynichese glyphs that can be interpreted as the substitution for a single

plaintext letter when referencing our cipher alphabets is interpreted as such. To include

this functionality, we define Alphabet 1 to end with Zattera’s slot 3, which features the

standalone glyphs <f>, <k>, <p>, and <t>. Seeing one of these glyphs means that the

glyph and everything to the left of it maps onto a single letter within Alphabet 1.

Similarly, Alphabet 2 is the only alphabet where glyph strings can begin with <ch>,

<sh>, <cfh>, <ckh>, <cph>, <cth>, or <e>.




                                                                                         24
       In the intentionally rare event that the cipher alphabets permit ambiguities in the

readings of a given token (e.g., if <qoiid> is meant to be parsed as <q> <oiid> or as

<qo> <iid>), the decoder writes down all possible readings of the token based on the

cipher alphabets and then uses context clues after the fact to select the correct reading.

       If any of the cipher alphabets happen to overlap in their glyph strings—which

we generally try to avoid—then we accept that we have inadvertently made our cipher

polyphonic, in which one ciphertext glyph string maps to 2 or more plaintext letters. In

this case, the decoder also writes down all possible readings of the questionable token

based on the cipher alphabets and uses context clues to select the correct reading.



S2.5. Population-level behavior of randomly generated Voynichesque variants

To elaborate on the argument set forth in Section 3.3 of the main text, there is no getting

around the general problem of trigram-rich plaintexts yielding ciphertexts whose word

type length distributions and entropy values do not reliably replicate the VMS at the

same time—so much so that I posit that the VMS’s word type length distribution places

a powerful constraint on any substitution cipher proposal for the VMS. If, for instance,

Voynichesque were altered so that it uses 4 3-glyph-long alphabets versus the 3 4-glyph

alphabets presented above, tensions would simply kick in with 4-grams and get worse

from there, while making it that much harder to reliably replicate the VMS’s entropy

values within the restrictions set by the word type length distribution.

       The VMS places stringent limits on overall word type length—less than 13

glyphs for all but extremely rare words, as calculated after Stolfi (2000)—and its

observed word grammar and entropy values indicate that glyphs are paired up more

predictably than alphabet letters are in natural languages (Zandbergen 2024), suggesting

that if the VMS is a ciphertext, the cipher is probably verbose (Fisk et al. 2024). There




                                                                                          25
is a limit on how many natural-language plaintext letters one can cram into the average

VMS token using a substitution cipher without causing the resulting ciphertext to have

either higher average entropies or higher average word type lengths than what is

observed in the VMS. Abbreviation ciphers are not a way out of this swamp, as the

plaintext letters that remain after abbreviation are by their very nature of higher

informational value than the word parts that were just abbreviated—a critical reason

why the abbreviations could be done at all (Gheuens 2024). Relying heavily on

abbreviations risks complicating the ability to achieve low conditional character entropy

across an entire ciphertext token, and therefore across an entire ciphertext.

       Even if an abbreviation cipher could be achieved that replicates various VMS

properties when applied to a given plaintext, it would present a serious fine-tuning

problem: How dependent would such a VMS-mimic cipher be on the diction of a

particular plaintext? Could it deliver VMS-like ciphertext reliably across a range of

plausible plaintexts within the same natural language? If not, then the immediate

corollary is that if the VMS had been encrypted using an abbreviation cipher, then the

probability of us seeing the VMS with the properties it has would have been low.

       Overall, Voynichesque suggests that if the VMS is a ciphertext, each token

probably represents a plaintext n-gram no more than ~6 letters long, with the vast

majority being 1–2 letters long. This result reproduces one of the tacit results of Hermes

(2022): that an artificial-language cipher that acts on a plaintext with many short n-

grams (or, in the case of Polygraphia III, entirely unigrams) can deliver broadly VMS-

like ciphertext behavior. However, with Voynichesque, this result emerges in a context

with a far closer resemblance to the VMS—and is validated to an extraordinary degree

by the performance of the Naibbe cipher.

Supplementary Material 3: Fitting the frequency-rank word type distribution of

Voynich B with a model of a substitution cipher acting on plaintext unigrams

Voynichesque suggests that if the VMS is encrypted with a substitution cipher, then one

favorable way to replicate the VMS’s properties is to break up the plaintext into mostly

unigrams and bigrams and then encrypt those unigrams and bigrams as ciphertext word

types on a letter-by-letter basis. If the VMS is encrypted using this general kind of

substitution cipher, Voynichesque also implies that the VMS’s commonest word types

may all represent plaintext unigrams. If so, these words’ frequency-rank distribution

would be expected to result from the average number of substitution options that can be

applied to a given plaintext alphabet letter, the average ratios in which the options are

applied, and the plaintext alphabet letter frequencies. I now describe a procedure by

which this scenario can be modeled and fitted to Voynich B’s observed frequency-rank

distribution of its commonest word types.

70 of the commonest Voynich B words can be plaintext unigrams in disguise.



S3.2. Results of fitting the cipher model to Voynich B’s 70 commonest word types

When my model is fitted to Voynich B’s proportional frequency-rank distribution of

word types, the letter frequencies that provide the best fit more closely resemble those

of Romance languages than of some non-Romance language families, such as Slavic

languages. The letter frequencies of Latin and late medieval Italian, as calculated from

Dante’s Divina Commedia, provide excellent fits.



S3.2.1. Initializing the model with a multi-language average

We begin by initializing our model with the average absolute letter frequencies of the

23 commonest letters of English, French, German, Czech, Spanish, Finnish, Portuguese,

Italian, Danish, Polish, Swedish, Dutch, and Icelandic after stripping those letters of




                                                                                          35
diacritics (Wikipedia 2025; S3 Tables 1 and 2). To initialize the model with inequality

in option application, we initialize the option ratios as r1 = 6, r2 = 5, r3 = 4, r4 = 3, r5 =

2, and r6 = 1. The model’s resulting best-fit letter frequencies tend to cluster with those

of Romance languages, with three of the top four closest matches being Portuguese,

Spanish, and Italian (S3 Tables 3–5).



S3.2.2. Initializing with Latin

Our first attempt to fit our model was based on letter frequencies averaged out across

multiple divergent European language groups. This is not reflective of our stylistic and

historical knowledge of the VMS, which places the VMS firmly within the tradition of

late medieval herbals found within northern Italy and Germany (Zandbergen 2024c),

many of which were written in Latin (D’Imperio 1978). When re-initialized with Latin

letter frequencies, our model converges on an excellent fit of six options per letter

applied in approximately 5:2:2:2:1:1 ratios, and the best-fit letter frequencies strongly

resemble those of modern Italian.

        I then calculated letter frequencies for the main text (i.e., removing canto titles)

of Dante’s Divina Commedia (Supplementary Table 6). When initialized with Latin and

Italian, model-estimated frequencies that best fit Voynich B closely match those in the

Divina Commedia, which outperforms all tested modern languages across all model

runs (S3 Tables 3–5).



S3.2.3. Initializing with German

When initialized with the German alphabet, the fitting procedure alters the letter

frequencies to more closely resemble Spanish and Portuguese, thereby reinforcing that

Latin and Romance languages provide better fits (Supplementary Table 2c). To generate




                                                                                             36
the best possible match to Voynich B, the three commonest letters in the model’s

plaintext alphabet need to occur in roughly 12:11:10 ratios, and the commonest letter

needs to have a frequency of 11–12%, which is observed in Latin and Italian and not in

German (S3 Tables 4 and 5).



S3.3. Universal recovery of Voynich B’s frequency-rank word type distribution

In every case where we fit our hypothetical cipher model against the proportional

frequency-rank word type distribution of Voynich B, we can recover the absolute

frequency-rank word type distribution of Voynich B by dividing our best-fit ciphertext

word frequencies almost exactly in half. In other words, we universally recover

Voynich B’s absolute word type distribution from our model of the proportional

distribution if we assume that Voynich B is a ciphertext whose plaintext is ~50%

unigrams. Our work with Voynichesque, as described in Supplementary Material 2,

suggests that nearly all the remaining ~50% of the hypothetical Voynich B plaintext

probably would consist of bigrams.

        The combination of the six substitution options, the approximate 5:2:2:2:1:1

ratios, the excellent fit to Latin and Italian alphabet letter frequencies, and the model’s

implied 50-50 split between plaintext unigrams and plaintext bigrams directly led to the

development of the Naibbe cipher as described in Section 2 of the main text. The

Naibbe cipher’s performance at mimicking the VMS when encrypting Latin and Italian,

as outlined in the main text’s Section 3, serves as a striking validation of this fit. This

modeling effort also represents an unusual test of the VMS’s compatibility with being a

ciphertext—one that Voynich B, remarkably, passes. There is no intrinsic reason why

the model’s best-fit alphabet letter frequencies must resemble those of natural

languages, much less those of Latin and Italian specifically.

Supplementary Material 4: Random walk analysis

       See Peng et al. (1992), Kokol et al. (1999), and Schinner (2007) for a more

thorough treatment of the generals of random walk analysis and its specific applications

to text, but in essence, this analysis assigns each alphabet letter within a text a unique,

5-digit code made of 0s and 1s (the exact codes do not matter that much), and then each

1 or 0 is treated as a “step” forward (1) or backward (-1), respectively, on a “walk.” If

this walk were truly random over the course of the entire binary sequence, then the

direction of any one step would be independent of previous steps. However, if long-

range correlations exist within a given binary sequence, the direction of a given step

depends on the directions of previous steps, amounting to a “memory” effect.

       Taking after Peng et al. (1992), let us define the walker’s ith step as resulting in

the unit step u(i), where u(i) = ±1. We also define y(l) as being the walker’s net

displacement after l steps:
                                               3

                                      𝑦(𝑙) = 7 𝑢(𝑖)
                                              !4+


For example, for the binary sequence {01101}, y(4) = 0, and y(5) = 1.

       Let us also define l0 as a binary sequence position that marks the start of l steps.

We define Δy(l) as:

                                𝛥𝑦(𝑙) = 𝑦(𝑙5 + 𝑙) − 𝑦(𝑙5 )



Returning to our example binary sequence {01101}, for l0 = 3, Δy(1) is given as:



                         𝛥𝑦(1) = 𝑦(4) − 𝑦(3) = 0 − 1 = −1

We also define the mean square fluctuation F2(l) as:

                              𝐹 . (𝑙) = AAAAAAAAAAA AAAAAAAC.
                                        [∆𝑦(𝑙)]. − B∆𝑦(𝑙)

Where a horizontal bar denotes average values across all positions l0. The root mean

square fluctuation F(l) is simply 𝐹(𝑙) = D𝐹 . (𝑙).

       As Peng et al. (1992) helpfully put it, what we are basically doing is fixing a set

of calipers of width l and then going down the binary sequence and treating each bit as

l0. Each time we stop at a given l0, we calculate Δy(l) and its square. In plain English,

F2(l) is the difference between the average of the squares of all values of Δy(l) and the

square of the average of all values of Δy(l). For a fully random walk, the root mean

square fluctuation F(l) in the displacement of a walk of l number of steps, each of length

±1, would be expected to approximate √𝑙, especially at large values of l. However, if

𝐹(𝑙) > √𝑙, then long-range correlations of some kind, and to some degree, are at work.
